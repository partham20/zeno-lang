\chapter{Keywords}\label{app-keywords}

These 37 words are the keywords of Zeno. A keyword is never a name: a
variable, function or field cannot be called \verb|match| or
\verb|take|, and the compiler says so if one is.

\section*{Words that are not keywords}

A few words have a meaning in one place and are ordinary names
everywhere else, because in that place no name could stand:

\begin{itemize}
\item \verb|test|, before a string at the top of a file, starts a test
  (\hyperref[ch-testing]{Testing}).
\item \verb|package|, \verb|needs| and \verb|at| are the words of a
  \verb|zeno.package| file (\hyperref[ch-packages]{Packages}).
\item The names of the built-in types (\verb|int|, \verb|str|,
  \verb|list|, \verb|u8| and the rest), of the units (\verb|ms|,
  \verb|V|) and of the built-in functions (\verb|print|, \verb|len|)
  are names declared by Zeno, which a program may declare again in an
  inner block.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Words from other languages}

These are not Zeno, and each gets a message with the Zeno spelling:
\verb|def| and \verb|fn| (\verb|function|), \verb|elif|
(\verb|else if|), \verb|&&|, \verb+||+ and \verb|!| (\verb|and|,
\verb|or|, \verb|not|), and a \verb|;| at the end of a line. So do the names other languages
give to nothing and to the two truths: \verb|None|, \verb|null|,
\verb|nil|, \verb|NULL|, \verb|nullptr| and \verb|undefined| are
told that Zeno spells it \verb|none|, and \verb|True| and
\verb|False| that it spells them \verb|true| and \verb|false|.
